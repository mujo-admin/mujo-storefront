How much protein do you actually need?

Seven in ten of us are actively trying to eat it. Eight in ten of us don't know what the target is. Here's the number, the slightly undignified way scientists worked it out, and when it makes sense to go above it.

By Kinga · 8 min read

Somewhere in the last few years, protein lost the plot.

There's protein popcorn now. Protein chips, protein cookies, protein granola, protein water. Somewhere along the way "contains protein" started to mean "good for you," and a nutrient became a personality.

The numbers back up the feeling. In a 2025 survey, 71% of Americans said they were trying to consume protein, up from 59% three years earlier. In a second survey by the same group, about eight in ten didn't know how much they're supposed to eat in a day.[1]

So most of us are sprinting towards a finish line we can't see. Let's go and find it.

The official number

For healthy adults, the US recommended dietary allowance for protein is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day.[2] In pounds, that's about 0.36 grams per pound.

Find your row and keep it in mind. I'll explain the other two columns in a minute.

Where 0.8 comes from (the undignified part)

Protein is the only major nutrient with nitrogen in it. That one fact lets scientists do something clever. If they measure all the nitrogen a person eats and all the nitrogen that leaves their body, they can tell whether that person is gaining protein, losing it, or holding steady.

"All the nitrogen that leaves" means what you think it means. Volunteers in these studies eat carefully measured diets for days while researchers collect and analyse everything. It is nobody's favourite experiment.

In 2003, three researchers pooled these studies: 235 people, 19 studies.[3] They found that the typical adult holds steady at about 0.66 grams of protein per kilogram a day. People vary, though, so the allowance was set higher, at the level that covers 97.5% of healthy adults. That's the 0.8.[2][3]

It helps to know what kind of number this is. One research group describes the allowance as the amount "to meet population needs and to prevent deficiency."[4] It tells you where the floor is for nearly everyone. It was never meant as a target for building muscle, or for being 70.

Now the twist: you're probably already there

Here's the thing the protein-popcorn aisle would rather you didn't know.

When researchers looked at what nearly 58,000 Americans eat, adults were averaging roughly 1.1 to 1.3 grams of protein per kilogram a day, with every age and demographic group above the average requirement.[5] (They measured against ideal body weight, so treat that as a ballpark.)

So the average American adult is already eating about one and a half times the baseline, mostly without trying. If you eat regular meals with some meat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans or tofu in them, there's a good chance you cleared your number today without thinking about it.

Which makes the real question a different one: who has a reason to aim higher than the floor?

When more makes sense

The research points to two groups in particular.

People who lift. A 2018 analysis pulled together 49 trials and 1,863 people doing resistance training. Extra protein did help them gain muscle and strength. But the benefit levelled off: past a total intake of about 1.6 grams per kilogram a day, more protein added nothing further.[6] That's the third column in the table, and it's a ceiling for one specific goal.

People over 65. An international group of ageing specialists reviewed the evidence in 2013 and recommended that older adults eat at least 1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilogram a day, and more if they're active.[7]

That's the middle column: a reasonable setting if you're active or getting older and want some margin above the floor.

And then there's everyone else, for whom the honest answer is that the floor is the floor, and most of us are comfortably above it.

Can you eat too much?

For healthy adults, the evidence is reassuring. A review of 28 trials and 1,358 people found that kidney function didn't change differently on higher-protein diets than on normal ones.[8]

The important exception is people who already have kidney disease, who may need to limit protein.[7] If that's you, or you're pregnant, or you're managing a health condition, your number is a conversation with your doctor and not something to take from a blog.

"Your body can only use 30 grams at a time"

You've probably heard this one. It's why people set alarms for protein shakes.

In 2023, researchers tested it directly. They gave people either 25 grams or 100 grams of protein in one sitting after a workout, and tracked where it went using labelled amino acids. The 100 grams wasn't wasted. The body's response was bigger and lasted more than 12 hours.[9]

A fair caveat: other researchers have pointed out that this was one study, in young men, after exercise, and it doesn't prove that timing never matters.[10] But the idea of a hard 30-gram cap doesn't hold up well.

The one timing idea worth keeping

If there's a pattern worth knowing about, it's the lopsided day. A lot of us eat very little protein at breakfast, a bit at lunch and most of it at dinner.

A small study had eight adults eat the same total protein two ways: spread evenly across three meals (about 30 grams each), or skewed (about 11, 16 and 63). Muscle protein synthesis over 24 hours was 25% higher on the even days.[4]

Eight people is a small study, so hold it loosely. Still, it points at something practical. If you want to change one thing, breakfast is usually where the room is.

So what do you do with all this?

Three steps, and none of them require an app.

  1. Find your row in the table above.
  2. Count one normal day. Just once, roughly. Most people are surprised in one direction or the other.
  3. If you're short, fix your lowest meal first. For most of us that's breakfast.

And you may well not need a protein powder to do any of it. Food comes first. A powder earns its place when it makes a low-protein meal easier to fix. Ours has 22 grams a serving and goes in a smoothie, for the mornings when eggs aren't happening.

Next time: the number on the tub

Let's say you do reach for a tub. You'll look at the big number on the front, and you'll assume somebody measured the protein.

They didn't, exactly. The standard test for the protein on a label doesn't measure protein. It measures something else and multiplies by 6.25.

FAQ

How much protein do I need per day? The US recommended dietary allowance for healthy adults is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight, or about 0.36 grams per pound. For a 140 lb adult that's about 51 grams.[2]

How much protein do I need to build muscle? In a 2018 analysis of 49 trials, protein intakes above about 1.6 grams per kilogram a day gave no further gains from resistance training.[6]

Do older adults need more protein? An expert group recommends at least 1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilogram a day for adults over 65.[7]

Can your body only absorb 30 grams of protein at once? A 2023 study found that 100 grams in one sitting produced a larger and longer response than 25 grams, which doesn't support a fixed cap.[9]

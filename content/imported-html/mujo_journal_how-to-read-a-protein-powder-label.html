How to read a protein powder label in two minutes

The big number on the front isn't measured the way you'd think. Here are six things to check on any tub, and then, because it's only fair, I'll run our own through the same list.

By Kinga · 9 min read

Last time I promised you a slightly unsettling fact about the number on the front of a protein tub. Here it is.

In most cases, nobody measures the protein directly.

Under US labelling rules, protein content can be worked out by measuring how much nitrogen is in the food and multiplying by 6.25.[1] It's a sensible shortcut, because protein is the main source of nitrogen in food, and it's been standard practice for a very long time.

But it means the test is counting nitrogen. It can't tell you which amino acids that nitrogen came from, or whether they're the nine your body needs. You learned in part two why that matters.

So the number on the front is a starting point. The useful information is on the back, and it takes about two minutes to read once you know where to look.

Stop 1: Which box is it?

Turn the tub round and find the panel with the numbers. Its title will be either Supplement Facts or Nutrition Facts, and that one word tells you which rulebook the product was made under.

A product with a Supplement Facts panel is a dietary supplement. The FDA is blunt about what that means: it "does not have the authority to approve dietary supplements before they are marketed."[2] A product with a Nutrition Facts panel is a conventional food and follows food labelling rules.

Neither kind gets tested and approved one product at a time, so this isn't a good-versus-bad split. It's useful mainly because of the next thing.

Supplement rules allow something called a proprietary blend: a group of ingredients listed under one name.[3] You're told what's in the blend but not how much of each. If the protein in a tub sits inside a blend, you can't work out what you're paying for.

Stop 2: The protein line (and the percentage next to it)

Three numbers matter here.

Grams per serving. Fine, but remember it's weight, not quality.

The % Daily Value beside it. This is the trick from part two. The percentage has to be corrected for protein quality, and it must be shown when a product makes a protein claim.[1] The Daily Value is 50 grams, so 20 grams of top-quality protein should read 40%.

Cost per gram of protein. Tubs come in wildly different sizes and serving sizes, so price per tub tells you nothing. Do this instead:

price ÷ (servings × grams of protein per serving)

It takes ten seconds on a phone and it's the only fair way to compare two products.

Stop 3: What's making it sweet?

Scan the ingredients for the sweetener. You'll usually find one of three kinds.

  • Sugar, in some form. It shows up in the sugar line of the panel.
  • Sugar alcohols. These mostly end in "-itol": sorbitol, maltitol, xylitol, erythritol. They're worth knowing about because a scientific review found they "may cause gastrointestinal disturbances," at least in people who aren't used to them. Erythritol was the exception and usually didn't.[4]
  • High-intensity sweeteners, such as sucralose, stevia or monk fruit. A tiny amount does the job, so they appear near the end of the list.

Which one you prefer is mostly taste. The point is to know which you're getting, especially if protein shakes have ever disagreed with you and you never worked out why.

Stop 4: The gums

Xanthan gum, guar gum, gellan gum, acacia, and emulsifiers like lecithin: these are the ingredients that keep a shake smooth instead of separating into sand and water.

"No gums" has become a selling point, so it's worth knowing what regulators have said. When Europe's food safety authority re-evaluated xanthan gum in 2017, it concluded there was "no safety concern for the general population" and that it didn't need a set daily limit. It also noted, fairly, that at high test doses "some individuals experienced abdominal discomfort." Those doses were around 214 mg per kilogram of body weight a day, which is roughly 15 grams for a 70 kg adult.[5]

So a gum on the label isn't a red flag. If you know your gut dislikes one, that's a good reason to check for it.

Stop 5: "Natural flavors"

This phrase sounds like it means "vanilla, from a vanilla pod." Legally it's much broader.

The federal definition covers any oil, essence, extract, distillate or "product of roasting, heating or enzymolysis" whose flavour comes from a plant, animal or fermentation source, and whose job in the food is flavouring.[6] So it tells you where the flavour started. It doesn't tell you which plant, or what was done to it on the way.

That's allowed, and nearly every flavoured product uses the term. Just read it as "flavouring from a natural source" and not as a list of ingredients.

Stop 6: The thing the label can't tell you

Heavy metals. This is the one that makes headlines, so let's go slowly.

In October 2025, Consumer Reports tested 23 protein powders and shakes. More than two-thirds had more lead in a single serving than the level its experts consider safe to have in a day. The plant-based products averaged about nine times the lead of the dairy-based ones.[7]

Before anyone panics, two things you need to know to read that properly.

First, why plants? Lead is in soil, and plants take it up as they grow. Concentrate a plant into a powder and you concentrate whatever came with it.[7]

Second, which yardstick? Consumer Reports used 0.5 micrograms of lead a day as its level of concern. That's the same figure California uses for its Proposition 65 warnings, and it's deliberately strict: it's set 1,000 times below the level at which no reproductive harm was observed.[8] The FDA uses a different benchmark for lead from food, 8.8 micrograms a day for women of childbearing age and 2.2 for children, which already includes a tenfold safety margin.[9]

Those two yardsticks are about 17 times apart. That's why one product can carry a California warning and sit well inside the FDA's level. Both facts can be true of the same tub.

None of this is on the label, so here's what you can do:

  • Ask whether every batch is tested by an outside lab. Not once a year. Every batch.
  • Ask to see results. A brand that tests can show you.
  • Read a Prop 65 warning for what it is. It means a product is above California's very strict line. It doesn't tell you by how much.

FAQ

How is the protein on a label measured? US rules allow protein to be calculated as 6.25 times the nitrogen content of the food.[1]

What is a proprietary blend? A group of ingredients on a supplement label listed under a single name, without the amount of each one.[3]

Is xanthan gum safe? Europe's food safety authority concluded in 2017 that there is no safety concern for the general population at reported levels of use.[5]

Why do some protein powders have a Prop 65 warning? California requires a warning above 0.5 micrograms of lead a day, a level set 1,000 times below the no-observed-effect level for reproductive harm. Plants take up lead from soil, so plant-based powders are more likely to cross that line.[7][8]
